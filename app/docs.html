Methodology / 2026 season / Chiliz Spicy

FSPI Tennis Index turns the ATP and WTA tours into a tradable market: every ranked player gets a token whose price is pinned to a transparent, fully-retraceable performance level.

0 · The simple version

The token is a jar. Every player token has its own CHZ jar. When you buy, your CHZ goes into the jar and fresh tokens are printed for you at the fair price. When you sell, your tokens are destroyed and CHZ comes back out of the same jar. There is always real money in the jar behind every token — nobody can be left holding nothing, because your sell doesn't need a buyer on the other side.

Points are a surprise scoreboard. Every player starts the season on 7,500 points. Before each match the engine asks: how likely was this player to win? Win when you were expected to lose, and the level jumps. Win when everyone expected it, and it barely moves. Lose as a heavy favourite, and it falls hard. A Grand Slam final counts more than a small event's first round; sets and games add a small adjustment. A player's level is 7,500 plus their season points.

The price is the level divided by 7,500. That's the whole formula. A player sitting at 7,500 costs 1 CHZ per token. If they beat expectations, their level rises and so does the price; if they fall short, both fall. So buying a token is a bet that the player will do better than the market expects — not simply that they will win. That is why an underdog can be the better buy: their wins are not priced in yet.

1 · What this is

Each player has an on-chain token (one per player, e.g. ATP-SIN for Jannik Sinner). The token mints and burns at a NAV (net asset value) derived from that player's level — a season surprise score computed by a deterministic engine from official match results. Buy a player you think will beat expectations; sell one you think won't.

Three properties make the design honest:

  • Backed 1:1. Every token in circulation is fully collateralized by CHZ held against it. Supply can only change by minting (buy) or burning (sell) at NAV.
  • Retraceable. Every level change maps to the exact matches that caused it — the UI exposes the trail, and the daily close is sha256-hashed so any number can be re-derived from the raw match file.
  • Capped updates. On-chain level moves are limited to ±25% per push, so a single data error can never gap a token's price.

2 · How indexing works

Season window

The 2026 book tracks matches from 2026-07-01 (mid-year book start) through December 31. On January 1 every level resets to the 7,500.00 base, and any reserve surplus above base levels is swept back to the core reserve. Positions carry into the new season at reset prices.

Data pipeline

  • Source. ESPN's public schedule/scoreboard feeds for ATP and WTA, plus ATP/WTA ranking snapshots that calibrate the opening ratings.
  • Backfill. The keeper fetches completed matches per day since the season start, normalizes rounds and event names, and stores raw records with ESPN competition IDs (compId) — the key for idempotency.
  • Watch loop. Every 15 minutes: pull new completed matches, apply them, push changed levels on-chain (batched, chunked), publish the Official Daily Close at 09:00 UTC, repair token fees, and top up the operator wallet from the Spicy faucet when low.

Eligibility rules

  • Only completed matches count; walkovers and retirements are excluded.
  • Only tour-level singles: team events (United Cup, Davis/BJK Cup, Laver Cup), exhibitions, Challengers and ITF are excluded outright (tier EXCL, multiplier 0).
  • Only recognized rounds: qualifying is skipped. ESPN labels early rounds "Round 1..4" without draw context — these are resolved per event using the event's named rounds (QF/SF/F) as anchors, falling back to match-count heuristics. Unresolvable rounds are skipped, never guessed.

3 · Scoring: performance vs expectation

Every player starts the season on 7,500.00. From there the level moves on surprise, not on win count. Before each match the engine computes an expected win probability from the two players' ratings:

p = 1 / (1 + 10^((rating_opponent − rating_you) / 400))
winner points = K × (1 − p_winner) × tier × round   (K = 200)
loser points  = −winner points
rating update = rating + 24 × (result − p)

Zero-sum by construction. The winner gains exactly what the loser gives: K × (1 − p). A match mints nothing. A favourite beating an underdog (p ≈ 0.9) moves the level by a few points; the same favourite losing moves it by roughly nine times as much; an underdog winning (p ≈ 0.2) captures most of the transfer. That is why buying an underdog can beat holding a constant winner — the favourite's wins are already in the price.

Milestones are the only new points. Each is multiplied by the tier and by the surprise of the decisive match — the player's pre-match win probability in the final (title / runner-up), the quarterfinal (semifinalist) or the round of 16 (quarterfinalist). A season scale of 0.25 keeps the total mint near zero. An underdog title (p = 0.2966) at a Masters earns 200 × 1.0 × 0.7034 × 0.25 = 35.17; the same title as a heavy favourite (p = 0.7034) earns 14.83.

4 · Worked example

The same 6-4, 6-3 win, two different expectations. A Masters (tier ×1.0) Round of 32 (round ×0.40), Player A rated 1,850 against Player B rated 2,000:

p(A wins) = 1 / (1 + 10^((2,000 − 1,850)/400)) = 0.2966 A wins (upset): base: 200 × (1 − 0.2966) × 1.0 × 0.4 = +56.27 stats: (2 sets × 4 + 5 games × 0.2) × 0.4 = +3.60 total: +59.87 B wins (expected): base: 200 × (1 − 0.7034) × 1.0 × 0.4 = +23.73 stats: +3.60 total: +27.33

The same scoreline pays 2.2× more when the underdog wins. The loser gives exactly the winner's points, so the match mints nothing. If A goes on to win the title with a 29.66% final expectation, the milestone is 200 × 1.0 × (1 − 0.2966) × 0.25 = 35.17; a 70.34% favourite winning the same title earns 14.83.

Every number is integer math in centi-points — deterministic and replayable from the match file.

5 · From level to NAV

A token's NAV (price per token in CHZ) is its level scaled by the base:

NAV = level ÷ 7,500    e.g. level 7,878.85 → NAV 1.0505 CHZ/token reserve target = supply × NAV

The core contract keeps every token fully backed: when a level rises, the target reserve rises and the keeper tops the difference up from the core pool; when it falls, the surplus is swept back. Reserve = supply × NAV holds at all times — the market page shows the live funded ratio.

6 · How trading works

There is no order book. Each player token is its own mint/burn pool pinned to NAV:

  • Buy — send CHZ to buy(minTokensOut). 1% fee is taken, the remainder buys at the current NAV. The token is minted to you.
  • Sell — call sell(tokenIn, minChzOut). Tokens burn at NAV, minus the 1% sell fee; CHZ goes to your wallet.
  • Slippage guard — the UI computes minOut at 2% below the on-chain NAV and reverts if the level moved against you mid-transaction.
  • Fee → core reserve — all trading fees accumulate in the core contract as the reserve buffer. It's the venue's only revenue, and it grows with volume, not with your losses.
  • Zero cold-start — because tokens mint on demand, a brand-new player token is instantly tradeable with zero idle capital. The first buyer sets the pool.
Season reset. On January 1, levels reset to 7,500.00 and each token's reserve is rebased; any surplus above the base level returns to the core reserve. Consider your positions before the reset — the UI shows a countdown in the last 21 days of the season.

7 · The Official Daily Close

Intraday level pushes are indicative — they can arrive out of order and are capped at ±25%. Once a day at 09:00 UTC, the keeper recomputes all levels from the complete eligible match set and publishes the Official Daily Close: the day, match count, player count and a sha256 hash of the full level table.

  • The close is the settlement print — what payouts, licensing deals and disputes reference.
  • It is retraceable: hash the level table from close-2026.json, or replay matches-2026.json through the published engine, and you get the same number.
  • The header badge on the market page shows the latest close (day + hash prefix).

10 · Limitations

  • Testnet demo: real contracts, real transactions, play-money CHZ from the Spicy faucet.
  • Data latency: ESPN feeds update on their schedule; live in-progress matches are not scored until complete.
  • Single oracle operator — the cap (±25%/push), pausable core and hash-chained closes are the current trust mitigations; a production deployment would add multi-operator oracles and threshold signing.
  • Intraday NAV is indicative; only the Official Daily Close is settlement-grade.
  • Expectation, not strength. The level measures surprise, not absolute quality. A dominant favourite can lose points while winning as expected; an underdog can gain more from one upset than a champion gains from a title defence. The opening rating is calibrated to the ranking snapshot; from then on it moves only through results.
  • Token coverage. 202 of the 677 tracked players have live tokens. A player outside the registered set can rank highly on the index before a token is deployed; the market table marks them "soon" and the detail page explains that they are not yet tradeable.
  • Not financial advice; no real-money value.

11 · How regulated futures (FSPI × CME) solve solvency

Our design borrows FutureSports' benchmark discipline: a published, retraceable index and a daily settlement print. The scoring itself is expectation-relative, so match results transfer points between players rather than minting them. The difference is the venue: on July 29, 2026 CME Group announced a partnership to list futures and options on FutureSports Performance Indexes, and on August 11, 2026 it announced the world's first index-based hockey futures (launched September 28, pending CFTC review) — monthly and quarterly cash-settled, in standard (10× index value) and micro (1/10) sizes, with central clearing and methodologies aligned to the IOSCO Principles for Financial Benchmarks.

Why a futures exchange never faces our "vault pays the winners" problem

With zero-sum match scoring the core is already closer to a clearinghouse than a bookmaker: it moves points from losers to winners and mints only the small, surprise-scaled milestones. The remaining directional risk sits in the tokens themselves, which are fully collateralized.

  • Two-sided handshakes. A futures contract is a bet between two traders. Every buyer (long) is matched with a seller (short), so the crowd is its own counterparty and the market is zero-sum. The exchange holds no directional book at all.
  • Margin + daily settlement. Traders post collateral before trading; positions are marked to market daily and cash moves from losers to winners at each mark (that is exactly what our Official Daily Close is the analogue of). Traders who can't cover their losses are liquidated; a member-funded default fund backstops the rest.
  • Role separation. FutureSports is the index administrator — it computes and publishes the benchmark (zero market risk, earns licensing/data fees). CME is the venue and clearer (fees, no directional risk). The traders take the risk — from each other. The league supplies data but does not participate in index determination or governance.

What this demo already shares / what to adopt next

  • Shared: the Official Daily Close (≈ daily mark-to-market), the ±25% push cap (≈ circuit breaker), published retraceable methodology, and zero-sum match scoring. Our reserve model is stricter than futures margin (100% collateralized); with surprise-based transfers the core mainly clears points and funds a small milestone mint.
  • Next: (1) add a short side — pair-mint LONG+SHORT so crowd P&L nets to zero and the core never pays directional profit; (2) settle fees and funding at the daily close; (3) hold an insurance buffer from fee income and add per-wallet position limits; (4) court natural hedgers (sponsors, arena operators, broadcasters) who create genuine two-sided flow; (5) adopt benchmark-administrator governance (published change procedures, independence) if the benchmark itself becomes the product.

Is a short side "just betting"?

With shorts, users pay users — that is true of every two-sided market, including CME's. What separates trading from betting is not who pays whom; it is structure and purpose:

  • Asset vs. wager. A continuously-priced token on a published performance index can be held, traded, and exited at any time at a market price. A bet is a fixed-odds wager on an outcome that settles once. Our tokens are already the former — even long-only.
  • Neutral house vs. house edge. An exchange earns the rake on volume and has no interest in your P&L. A bookmaker sets the odds and profits when you lose. Long-only reserve tokens make the venue the counterparty — structurally closer to a bookmaker; adding shorts makes it neutral — closer to an exchange or a peer-to-peer betting market.
  • Hedgers vs. punters. Markets with real-world exposures to offset (sponsors, broadcasters, arena operators) are risk transfer; markets with only directional speculators are wagering. CME's launch materials are explicit that hedgers are the target.
  • Rulebook. Financial benchmarks (IOSCO principles) and derivatives regulation versus gaming regulation. Same economics, different wrapper — and regulators are actively contesting exactly this line for event contracts.

12 · The build path for a small team

Every market product has three roles: the scorekeeper (methodology, oracle, settlement print — FutureSports), the venue (matching, margin, licenses, capital float — CME), and the crowd (traders and hedgers). Licensing and capital are the venue's requirements; engineering is the scorekeeper's. This project occupies the scorekeeper slot and rents the venue later.

  • Stage 0 — testnet lab (live). Play-money on Chiliz Spicy. Proves the methodology, accumulates hash-chained closes, costs faucet gas. Zero regulatory surface.
  • Stage 1 — free-to-play, closed-loop. Same contracts; the money leg becomes an in-game chip with no cash-out. The venue issues its own currency, so the solvency problem disappears and reserve backing becomes optional (NAV remains the pricing rule). Monetize with passes, cosmetics and sponsored prize pools. The Sorare precedent is the cautionary case: cash-out is what triggers gambling classification.
  • Stage 2 — benchmark licensing. A season of verifiable closes is an asset: license the feed and record to fantasy apps, media, analytics — or to a licensed venue as its settlement benchmark. Zero market risk, the FutureSports slot.
  • Stage 3 — real-money leg, on someone else's license. If proven, integrate with a licensed exchange/sportsbook: they hold user funds and carry the licenses; we supply scores and settlement prints. The pair-mint short-side design waits in the drawer for this day.

Known risks: athlete names are facts but likeness merchandising needs counsel; ESPN public feeds are adequate for consumer products but an institutional benchmark needs league data rights; a trading UI without engagement flow dies quietly — Stage 1 is a game first, market second; FutureSports currently covers NHL/MLB teams, but tennis athletes remain a watch item.
